\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model validation}\label{sec:res-valid}
\emph{Element model.} Our implementation of~\eqref{eq:gamma} with the published parameters
reproduces the dispersion reported in~\cite{iudice2026} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dispersion}): differential
phase $172^\circ$--$184^\circ$ across 98\,MHz (reported $180^\circ\pm6.5^\circ$), magnitude imbalance
$\pm0.57$\,dB (reported $<0.75$\,dB), per-state phase variation $46.5^\circ$/$49.2^\circ$
(reported $\approx47^\circ$/$50^\circ$), ripple 0.62/0.50\,dB (reported $\approx0.6$\,dB).

\emph{Coupling model.} For $K=2$ the coupling follows the $\mathrm{sinc}(h/2)$ envelope with even
harmonics exactly zero for an ideal element; the dispersive cell creates a DC harmonic at
$-19$ to $-27$\,dB relative to $h=1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:harmonics}). The closed form~\eqref{eq:io}
agrees with an independent time-domain simulation (per-element filtering, sampled gating, FFT;
$Q=4$, $K=4$ random patterns, multipath on both hops) to 0.27\% median and 0.49\% maximum
relative error, the residual being the sampling of the gates.

\emph{Energy collection.} Corollary~\ref{cor:parseval} predicts the gain of joint equalization
over the one-tap receiver as $(2D-1)^{-2}$. Measured with the dispersive cell (Q=16, K=8,
30 channels, Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}): 11.6, 5.9 and 2.4\,dB at $D=0.625$, 0.75 and 0.875 against
predicted 12.0, 6.0 and 2.5\,dB. At 20\,dB with perfect knowledge, the one-tap rate is 0.10,
0.42 and 1.19\,bit/s/Hz at these duty cycles, the joint MMSE rate 6.14, 6.19 and 6.11, and the
matched-filter bound 6.45, 6.52 and 6.43. Joint MMSE recovers about 95\% of the matched-filter
bound. With random per-element shifts the matrix is worse conditioned and joint MMSE reaches
5.13 against a bound of 6.62\,bit/s/Hz at $D=0.75$.

\subsection{Value of not needing the element model}\label{sec:res-model}
Table~\ref{tab:model} compares receivers that differ only in what they assume about the
element (identical patterns, two pilot symbols plus two DD iterations, loss in dB against
perfect knowledge; fresh seed, 30 channels, 95\% CI on the rate loss $\le\pm0.05$\,bit/s/Hz).
A model-aware estimator with the exact model is excellent, but its accuracy collapses at high
SNR as soon as the model is wrong: a 10\,MHz resonance error costs 2.4\,dB at 30\,dB, and the
frequency-flat $\pm1$ model of earlier TM-RIS work costs 6.6\,dB. The timing-only estimator,
which uses no element model at all, is within 0.04--0.21\,dB at every SNR
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). This is the practical core of Corollary~\ref{cor:nomodel}: the
receiver needs only the switching timing.

\emph{Could the receiver fit the element model instead?} A natural alternative is to assume the single-pole
family and fit its six parameters (resonance and two decay rates per state) from the pilots. Starting from parameters
off by $\mathcal N(0,10\,{\rm MHz})$ and $\mathcal N(0,10\%)$, we maximized the evidence of the model-aware LMMSE over the
six parameters (30 channels, Table~\ref{tab:param}). With the correct family, the fitted model is no better than the
timing-only estimator ($+0.012\pm0.022$ and $+0.079\pm0.047$\,bit/s/Hz worse at 20/30\,dB); when the elements are not
identical (10\,MHz spread), the fit fails ($+0.73\pm0.43$ at 30\,dB) while the timing-only estimator is unaffected. The
model-aware estimators use the linear (full delay-matrix) form; part of the timing-only estimator's advantage at 30\,dB
comes from its low-rank step, which the model-aware form could also use.

\subsection{Is the result an artefact of the model? Independent truth and model mismatch}\label{sec:res-robust}
The ground truth~\eqref{eq:io} and the estimator's model share the same algebra (Proposition~\ref{prop:sep}), so
a reader may suspect an ``inverse crime''. We therefore ran the estimator \emph{unchanged} on data that do not
come from~\eqref{eq:io} or that deliberately violate its structure (30 channels each, paired against the same
channel without the change; pre-registered criterion: loss within 0.3\,bit/s/Hz).

\emph{Independent ground truth.} (i) Received pilots from a sampled-gating time-domain simulation (per-element
filtering at the absolute frequency, gating per slot, FFT; $Q=16$, $K=8$, multipath on both hops), scored on the
matrix obtained by probing the simulator; it differs from~\eqref{eq:io} by $5.6\cdot10^{-3}$ (relative Frobenius
norm). (ii) The element's switching transients, which~\eqref{eq:io} (a quasi-static model) omits: for a single-pole
cell the internal state is continuous at each switch and decays with the new state's pole, which adds
$H_{\rm tr}(\bar m,m)=\sum_q a^{\rm d}_q(f_{\bar m})\sum_{\text{edges }k}\frac{2\xi_{r,\rm new}}{T_u}G_{\rm new}(f_{\bar m})
e^{-j2\pi(\bar m-m)\Delta f t_k}[G_{\rm old}(f_m)-G_{\rm new}(f_m)]a^{\rm s}_q(f_m)$ with $G_s(f)=(j2\pi(f-f_{0,s})+\xi_s)^{-1}$; its
relative size is $1.3\cdot10^{-3}$. The losses are $+0.006\pm0.009$ (time domain) and $+0.003\pm0.006$\,bit/s/Hz (transients)
at 30\,dB.

\emph{Physics outside the model} (Table~\ref{tab:mismatch}). A state-dependent output response, mutual coupling that
depends on the states of \emph{both} neighbouring elements, frequency-dependent array phases (beam squint) and
per-element resonance spread change $\bm H$ by up to 40\%, yet the loss stays within $+0.05$\,bit/s/Hz everywhere. We had
predicted that joint-state coupling would break the model for per-element patterns; it does not at these levels,
because the per-group factors absorb it. State-independent coupling is absorbed exactly by construction (it only
redistributes the output responses).

\subsection{Estimators and baselines}\label{sec:res-est}
\emph{Against the literature's estimators.} Table~\ref{tab:est} and Fig.~\ref{fig:est} compare
estimators on the same pilots and channels (20 channels, $M=256$). The one-tap receiver is
capped at its interference ratio (Corollary~\ref{cor:parseval}). The Toeplitz model of the ideal
element in its usual form $H_{\bar mm}=t(\bar m-m)c(m)$ reaches 2.3/1.4\,bit/s/Hz: dispersion breaks its structure
(a much stronger ideal-element baseline, with our two-sided delay prior, is in Table~\ref{tab:fair}). CE-BEM with up to $\pm12$
harmonics reaches at most 2.3/1.4 and barely improves with pilots: by Corollary~\ref{cor:tail} it
misses 3.4\% of the coupling energy at $\pm12$. It even degrades from 20 to 30\,dB, because an
MMSE filter that trusts a wrong model hurts more when noise no longer masks the model error.
The proposed estimator is within 0.09\,bit/s/Hz (20\,dB) and 0.24\,bit/s/Hz (30\,dB) of perfect
knowledge with two pilot symbols.

\emph{Strongest baselines.} A reviewer may object that CE-BEM failed only because it was
truncated. Table~\ref{tab:bem} gives CE-BEM with up to all 255 harmonics and up to 32 pilot
symbols (20 channels; all harmonics on 4 channels because of its 8687 unknowns). Full-harmonic
CE-BEM does approach our result, but only with 32 pilot symbols (6.18/9.37\,bit/s/Hz): the
proposed estimator with two pilot symbols (6.51/9.69) is better than full CE-BEM with 32, which
still falls 0.41/0.52\,bit/s/Hz short of perfect knowledge. The correct statement is therefore that
ours with two pilot symbols exceeds full CE-BEM with 16 times more pilots; CE-BEM did not reach
within 0.3\,bit/s/Hz of perfect knowledge at any pilot count tested. At equal overhead ($N_p=2$) full CE-BEM collapses to 0.13/0.08, because its
8687 unknowns cannot be determined from 512 observations. Sparse recovery (OMP) behaves like
truncated CE-BEM (2.32/1.19), and unstructured banded least squares is worst even with 32 orthogonal
pilot symbols (0.85/0.54).

\emph{Pre-registered fair rerun.} Tables~\ref{tab:est} and~\ref{tab:bem} give every baseline the naive MMSE
equalizer. Table~\ref{tab:fair} repeats the comparison under rules fixed before the run: 30 channels per point, both
the shared-path channel and 3GPP TDL-C at 100\,ns, $N_p\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}$, CE-BEM with no harmonic cap (matrix-free
LMMSE, checked against the dense solver to $7\cdot10^{-9}$), and three equalizers for \emph{every} estimator: naive
MMSE, robust MMSE with the estimation-error power taken from the pilot residual (usable in practice), and robust MMSE
with the oracle error power. The Toeplitz baseline is replaced by the strongest ideal-element estimator we could build:
the model-aware LMMSE with the ideal $\pm1$ element, $\bm H=(\bm T_1-\bm T_2)\circ(\bm F\bm C\bm F^{\mathsf T})$, the
same prior as ours. Table~\ref{tab:fair} reports the loss to perfect knowledge with the residual-based robust
equalizer; the oracle equalizer changes no conclusion (it helps truncated CE-BEM and OMP by up to 1.5\,bit/s/Hz at
30\,dB, and ours by at most 0.12).

The ranking survives. Ours is the only estimator that comes within 0.3\,bit/s/Hz of perfect knowledge at 20\,dB with
at most 32 pilot symbols: it needs $N_p=2$ on the shared-path channel and $N_p=4$ on TDL-C; no baseline gets there
with 32 (Table~\ref{tab:features}). The best baseline at every point is the ideal-element LMMSE: it loses only
0.4--0.5\,bit/s/Hz at 20\,dB, so the ideal element is a fair approximation when noise dominates, but 2.0--2.3 at
30\,dB, where the dispersion it ignores becomes the dominant error, and more pilots do not help it (a model error,
not an estimation error). Against the best baseline \emph{per channel} (minimum over the six baselines and three
equalizers, paired over channels), ours gains $0.41\pm0.02$ / $2.16\pm0.04$\,bit/s/Hz at 20/30\,dB with $N_p=2$ on the
shared-path channel and $0.30\pm0.06$ / $1.14\pm0.25$ on TDL-C, and $0.39$--$0.43$ / $0.54$--$2.15$ at all other pilot
counts; every interval excludes zero. Uncapped CE-BEM reaches the ideal-element LMMSE only with 32 pilot symbols
(loss 0.43 at 20\,dB on the shared-path channel), so ``ours with two pilot symbols is better than full CE-BEM with 32''
also holds under the fair rules. With the estimated-gain detector (the receiver does not know $G_{mm}$) every loss
grows by at most 0.15\,bit/s/Hz and the ranking is unchanged.

One pre-registered sanity check failed: the oracle-robust rate of the \emph{truncated} CE-BEM sets
($|h|\le12$, $|h|\le40$) still decreases from 20 to 30\,dB on the shared-path channel, by up to 0.28\,bit/s/Hz.
A scalar error power cannot represent a truncation error that is concentrated on the missing harmonics, so scalar
robustification does not fully remove the effect described above; the uncapped sets behave as expected (their rate
increases with SNR), and the check passes on TDL-C.

\emph{Equalizers.} Table~\ref{tab:eq} feeds the same estimate, and the true channel, to
equalizers from the high-mobility OFDM literature. Banded MMSE fails even with the true channel:
a $\pm32$ band given the true channel reaches 6.21 against 9.89\,bit/s/Hz for the dense MMSE at 30\,dB. This is
not only the harmonic tail: with smooth switching edges, whose tail is much shorter, the banded equalizers do not
recover (Table~\ref{tab:rise}). At $D=0.75$ three quarters of the energy is off the diagonal, and even a few percent
of it outside the window caps the SINR at high SNR. The iterative matrix-free solver matches the dense MMSE in 10
iterations, which justifies Algorithm~\ref{alg:cg}.

\emph{Dependence on the switching edges.} Table~\ref{tab:rise} repeats the comparison with a linear switching ramp
in the truth (and in our calibrated kernels). CE-BEM's failure depends strongly on sharp edges: with a ramp of 0.3 slot
(1.25\,$\mu$s) CE-BEM with $|h|\le40$ and 8 pilot symbols reaches 8.2\,bit/s/Hz, close to ours with 2 (8.56). For
realistic rise times up to 0.05 slot (about 200\,ns) our advantage stays above 4\,bit/s/Hz at 30\,dB. The advantage over
truncated estimators is therefore a property of fast switching, which the emission analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:res-hw} argues should be slowed to about 0.015 slot or more; we report it as such.

\emph{Fair-case check.} Earlier TM-IRS designs choose the pattern so that the intended user sees
little coupling. Table~\ref{tab:designed} uses per-element polarities that co-phase the $h=0$
harmonic at the user. Even then the one-tap receiver saturates at its interference ratio
(3.47\,bit/s/Hz at 30\,dB for $D=0.875$, interference ratio $+10.0$\,dB), while the proposed

\subsection{Full band}\label{sec:res-full}
At $M=3276$ all estimation and equalization use only the FFT operator. With the linear
first stage alone ($2L^2=1800$ unknowns) the estimate is noise-limited at $-23$ to $-29$\,dB at
28\,dB SNR (it reaches $-45$ to $-49$\,dB at 60\,dB SNR, so the model is right); the low-rank form
reaches $-39$\,dB with two pilot symbols. Table~\ref{tab:full} gives SER over 30 independent channels, with
95\% intervals of the SER \emph{ratio} to perfect knowledge from a clustered bootstrap (channels resampled,
$10^4$ resamples). With pilots only, the proposed receiver's SER is 1.10 [1.06, 1.15] times that of perfect knowledge
at 20\,dB/16-QAM and 1.37 [1.18, 1.86] times at 28\,dB/64-QAM; one DD pass brings these to 1.05 [1.03, 1.08] and 1.12
[1.08, 1.21]. (An earlier 10-channel run gave 1.54 at 28\,dB pilots-only; the 30-channel interval supersedes it.) Both
numbers are reported because the DD figure alone would overstate the pilots-only performance. A second DD pass made
64-QAM worse in an earlier run (decision errors feed back), so we use at most one. With per-element cyclic shifts, the
shift structure (Corollary~\ref{cor:groups}) gives an operator error of $-34.4$\,dB at 28\,dB and SER ratios of 1.10 and
1.12 (5 channels).

\subsection{Coded performance}\label{sec:res-coded}

\subsection{Sensitivity to design parameters}\label{sec:res-sweep}
Every parameter we chose was varied one at a time around the default (12 channels per point,
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). The gap to perfect knowledge stays at 0.06--0.09 / 0.10--0.20\,bit/s/Hz
(20/30\,dB) for $Q=4$ to 144 elements, 0.06--0.09 / 0.11--0.16 for $K=2$ to 16 slots, and 0.05--0.13 /
0.12--0.23 for duty cycles 1/8 to 7/8. The one parameter that matters is the number $G$ of
\emph{distinct} patterns: with two pilot symbols the gap grows from 0.07 / 0.10 at $G=1$ to 0.32 / 1.03 at
$G=16$, because the unknowns grow with $Gr^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:unknowns}); four pilot symbols bring it to
0.19 / 0.38. This gives a design rule: use four pilot symbols when the surface uses many distinct
per-element patterns, as security-oriented designs do.
